# Pore viral content research and replication playbook

Research date: July 14, 2026  
Primary goal: turn social reach into qualified Pore waitlist signups

## The answer in one sentence

Pore should not behave like another skincare education page. It should behave like a camera-first skincare decision show: start with a person, a shelf, a doubt, or a visible problem; use Pore as the proof mechanism; reveal one clear routine decision; then invite the viewer to try it.

Pore has the same underlying product-content fit that helped Cal AI: one camera action can create a visible, surprising result in seconds. The contentable Pore sequence is:

> face photo -> three visible cues -> product shelf -> one routine change -> progress over time

The weak version is “AI-personalized skincare.” The strong version is “I stopped guessing which of these products belonged on my face.”

## How this research was judged

“Viral” is treated as a breakout relative to the account’s normal performance, not just a large raw view count. Public performance figures below were captured from live third-party analytics pages and case studies on July 14, 2026. They can include paid distribution unless the source explicitly says organic. Platform-reported views are not the same as waitlist signups.

The most useful evidence has one or more of these qualities:

- A post materially outperformed the account’s usual reach.
- The product appears naturally inside an existing creator format.
- The first frame communicates tension without requiring brand familiarity.
- The product output is visually legible on a phone.
- The comments or story imply product intent, not only entertainment.

## Breakout content worth studying

### 1. Cal AI: the product is a six-second video

[Cal AI’s 2026 conference case study](https://blog.funnelfox.com/cal-ai-influencer-marketing/) reports 300+ active influencer partnerships, creator videos reaching 43M, 56M, and 73M organic views, and an early benchmark of roughly 10 installs per 1,000 views. The founders say detailed, product-intent comments were a better conversion signal than follower count or views alone.

Earlier examples include:

- [Kadin Kerns’ Cal AI integration](https://www.tiktok.com/@kadin_kerns/video/7400386546773314859), reported at about 2M views.
- [AyeJeremiah’s “don’t let me catch you cheating on your diet” skit](https://www.tiktok.com/@ayejeremiah/video/7379631538855546158), also reported at about 2M views.
- A “what 100 calories looks like” comparison from competitor Calo Scan, which made the app output the payoff to a curiosity hook.

The important lesson is not calorie content. It is product-content fit: creators were already filming meals, transformations, and “what I eat” videos, so the app became a natural tool inside their normal story. The [Shortimize case study](https://www.shortimize.com/blog/cal-ais-marketing-strategies-lessons-from-a-400k-mrr-success-story) documents those early posts and Cal AI’s creator/referral model.

**Pore translation:** creators are already filming GRWMs, shelf tours, product empties, acne diaries, and “what changed my skin” stories. Pore should be the decision layer inside those videos, not the subject of a standalone app commercial.

### 2. Rhode: make the product a social object

[Rhode’s four-image Spotwear carousel](https://www.instagram.com/p/DWy9w_Cle20) recorded about 58.6M impressions, 2.35M likes, and 3.2K comments in the public analytics snapshot. It was roughly 26 times the account’s stated average impressions per post.

The carousel is almost aggressively simple:

1. Candid flash photo of Hailey and Justin Bieber wearing tiny patches.
2. A closer, stranger face crop that makes the patches impossible to miss.
3. Justin taking a selfie, turning the product into part of his identity.
4. Another imperfect backstage-looking selfie.

There is no product packshot and almost no education on the slides. The product is wearable, recognizable, playful, and attached to a relationship viewers already care about. The caption carries the clinical/product information and the exact launch date. Performance source: [Rhode Instagram analytics](https://socialpruf.com/instagram/rhode).

[“When he uses eye prep”](https://www.tiktok.com/@rhode/video/7580174498763181343) used an attractive male model, oversized eye patches, and a three-word desirability joke. It recorded about 6.54M views and 968K likes, or roughly a 14.8% like-to-view rate. Performance source: [Rhode TikTok analytics](https://socialpruf.com/tiktok/rhode).

**Pore translation:** the face scan should be something friends, couples, roommates, and creators do to each other. “Scan my boyfriend,” “my mom rates the routine Pore made me,” and “the friend who owns six exfoliants” are more social than “watch our AI analyze a face.”

### 3. Sarah Palmyra: skepticism creates the open loop

[“There’s no way ear seeding actually works”](https://www.tiktok.com/@sarahpalmyra/video/7546684667739507981) recorded about 30.5M views and 1.15M likes, more than 70 times the account’s stated average views per post. The first frame shows a human reaction, a hand-on-face demonstration, and the skeptical quote in large text. The viewer stays to see whether the skepticism is resolved. Performance source: [Sarah Palmyra TikTok analytics](https://socialpruf.com/tiktok/sarahpalmyra).

The medical claim in that post should not be copied. The structure should:

> “There’s no way [simple action] can produce [specific, visible decision]” -> test -> reveal -> reaction

**Pore translation:** “There’s no way one face photo can clean up this shelf.”

### 4. James Welsh: strong opinions beat generic education

[“e.l.f. have f*cked up”](https://www.tiktok.com/@james_s_welsh/video/7538074304240504086) recorded about 2.57M views, 225K likes, and 3.8K comments, around 24 times the account’s average views. The first frame contains a face, a recognizable brand, a consequence, and an implied controversy. Performance source: [James Welsh TikTok analytics](https://socialpruf.com/tiktok/james_s_welsh).

His more constructive [“all you need is consistency, not new skincare”](https://www.tiktok.com/@james_s_welsh/video/7625370898933091606) still reached about 995K views and 68.7K likes. This is especially aligned with Pore’s progress and routine-adherence promise.

**Pore translation:** use strong but supportable decisions: “Your routine does not need another serum,” “these are good products in the wrong routine,” or “the expensive product is not the problem.” Do not manufacture outrage or attack a brand without evidence.

### 5. The Ordinary: give the creator a character and premise

[Jordan Smikle’s paid Markup Marché skit](https://www.tiktok.com/@jordansmikle/video/7634258574666894623) starts with “POV: that one friend who’s always buying something random.” It recorded about 8.69M views. The Ordinary’s message about deceptive markups sits inside an existing friend-character sketch rather than a product lecture. Performance source: [Jordan Smikle TikTok analytics](https://socialpruf.com/tiktok/jordansmikle).

The low like-to-view ratio relative to the best organic examples is a warning: paid reach can make a format look more culturally resonant than it is. Copy the premise, not the raw view count.

**Pore translation:** “POV: your friend buys every viral serum but still has no routine.” Pore becomes the punchline by showing product overlap and a simpler AM/PM plan.

### 6. CeraVe: culture in front, authority in the background

[“Only thing greener than the bottle is the flag”](https://www.tiktok.com/@cerave/video/7526964277396196621) recorded about 60.4M views and 763K likes in the public snapshot. CeraVe’s account also uses product characters, celebrity/sports partnerships, creator routines, and skin-camera content. Performance source: [CeraVe TikTok analytics](https://socialpruf.com/tiktok/cerave).

The [July 2026 skincare scan](https://lightreel.ai/blogs/skincare-brands-on-tiktok) characterizes CeraVe’s strongest current approach as meme-first, with dermatologist credibility functioning as a trust layer rather than the first-frame hook.

**Pore translation:** never begin with “advanced computer vision” or “science-backed AI.” Begin with the human tension, then let capture quality, consistent lighting, ingredient logic, and careful language supply credibility.

### 7. UMAX: outputs become shareable identity tokens

[Fortune’s UMAX report](https://fortune.com/2024/07/01/looksmaxxing-apps-rate-teen-boys-faces-mental-health/) says the app had passed 7M downloads and 1B social impressions as of its reporting. Users put app screenshots behind glow-up videos and used scores to quantify change. The visual output gave users something easy to post and debate.

That growth came with serious mental-health and self-image criticism. Pore should copy shareability, not attractiveness scoring, shame, or insecurity monetization.

**Pore translation:** share a neutral “routine changed” card, a consistency streak, a product-fit explanation, or a same-lighting progress comparison. Do not grade a person’s face.

### 8. Current SkinTok: transformation still works when it is immediate

A July 2026 creator-data post identified a [13-second before/after](https://www.tiktok.com/@mery.sebast/video/7648337919349951765) with the hook “the change my skin needed” at about 3.8M views. This is third-party observational evidence, not a first-party platform report, but it reinforces the pattern: the viewer understands the promised change before hearing an explanation.

**Pore translation:** when real longitudinal user data exists, start on the comparison and then reveal the routine decision. Match lighting, angle, expression, camera, and timing, and clearly state that individual results vary.

## What consistently works across the winners

### 1. Tension before information

The first frame is a doubt, mistake, cost, conflict, desire, or transformation. “My skincare routine” is not enough. “I own $280 of skincare and still don’t know what goes first” is.

### 2. A visible payoff

The viewer can see the scan, result chip, simplified routine, conflict warning, shelf reduction, progress comparison, or creator reaction. Abstract personalization is difficult to share.

### 3. The product has a job inside an existing story

Cal AI has a job in “what I eat.” Pore needs a job in GRWMs, shelf tours, empties, acne diaries, product reviews, and routine resets.

### 4. Creator identity stays intact

The strongest integrations look like a post the creator would have made anyway. The creator receives a premise and a proof moment, not a corporate script.

### 5. The first frame looks human

Faces, hands, shelves, messy bathrooms, flash photos, and native captions outperform a logo animation as an opener. Pore’s polished UI should be the reveal, not the cold open.

### 6. The content is a system

Cal AI scaled formats through hundreds of creators. One viral post is luck; one premise that works for five creators and ten hooks is a growth system.

### 7. Views and conversion are different

Cal AI’s team explicitly evaluates product-intent comments. Pore should distinguish “cute,” “glow,” and emojis from “what products did it remove?”, “does it work for sensitive skin?”, and “where do I try this?”

The [official TikTok beauty playbook](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/English_SMB_Beauty_META_Playbook_English_Final.pdf) recommends a simple structure: a 3–5 second hook, 10–20 seconds of value, and a clear 3–5 second close. Its suggested beauty narratives include product reveals, community results, formulator POVs, expectation-versus-reality, routines, vlogs, and scrollable lists. Pore should use those containers while keeping most initial tests between 9 and 18 seconds.

## The Pore content pillars

Use these five pillars to keep the account recognizable without making every post identical.

1. **Scan reactions:** a real person doubts, takes the guided photo, and reacts to one clear result.
2. **Shelf decisions:** Pore explains what stays, what moves, what overlaps, and what the routine is missing.
3. **Routine stories:** morning, night, gym, travel, sensitive-day, date-night, or “I’m late” routines with a job to do.
4. **Progress proof:** adherence, same-lighting comparisons, routine changes, and “what I stopped doing.”
5. **Culture and community:** friend archetypes, couple scans, comment replies, creator challenges, memes, and early-access stories.

Recommended mix for the first 30 posts:

- 10 scan reactions
- 8 shelf decisions
- 5 routine stories
- 4 progress stories
- 3 culture/community posts

## Ready-to-shoot video 1: the skeptical shelf test

Target length: 12–15 seconds  
Reference mechanisms: Sarah Palmyra skepticism, Cal AI one-snap demo

| Time | Visual | On-screen text / dialogue |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0–1.5s | Creator holds an overfilled skincare basket beside their face. | “There’s no way one photo can clean up this shelf.” |
| 1.5–3.0s | Fast guided-photo capture. Keep the real camera UI visible. | “Okay, prove me wrong.” |
| 3.0–5.5s | Pore result sheet rises: three visible-cue chips. | “It noticed what I’ve been tracking.” |
| 5.5–8.5s | Add three real products. Show one overlap/compatibility explanation. | “And these two did not need the same night.” |
| 8.5–11.5s | Clean AM/PM routine card. Creator removes products from frame. | “My routine went from chaos to this.” |
| 11.5–14.0s | Face + app, no logo-only end card. | “Pore is opening early access. Join the waitlist.” |

Caption:

> I thought I needed more skincare. I needed a routine I could actually understand. Pore starts with a guided face photo, then considers your goals and products to help organize a clear routine. It does not diagnose skin conditions. Join the early-access waitlist in bio.

Shoot three hook variants without changing the body:

- “My skincare shelf has no idea what it’s doing.”
- “I own seven serums and use none of them correctly.”
- “I asked a photo to tell me which products were fighting.”

## Ready-to-shoot video 2: the shelf cut

Target length: 9–12 seconds  
Reference mechanisms: before/after transformation, score/reveal apps

| Time | Visual | On-screen text / dialogue |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0–1.0s | Nine products slam onto the counter. | “$___ of skincare.” |
| 1.0–2.0s | Creator looks at camera. | “Zero routine.” |
| 2.0–5.0s | Three fast Pore clips: scan, add products, compatibility. | No voiceover; use clicks and native sound. |
| 5.0–8.5s | Match-cut to three products organized AM/PM. | “Cleanser. Treatment. SPF.” |
| 8.5–11.0s | Pore routine card beside the products. | “Stop buying. Start tracking. Waitlist in bio.” |

Use the creator’s real total and real products. Do not invent a conflict for drama.

## Ready-to-shoot video 4: “when he uses Pore”

Target length: 7–10 seconds  
Reference mechanism: Rhode’s “when he uses eye prep”

1. Open on a partner/friend looking unusually put together, not on the app.
2. Text: “When he has an actual skincare routine.”
3. One-second flashback: guided Pore scan.
4. One-second flash: simple AM routine card.
5. Return to the reaction shot.
6. Caption does the selling: “He stopped asking which bottle goes first.”

This should feel like a joke viewers send to someone, not a beauty ad.

## Ready-to-post carousel 1: “I thought I needed more skincare”

Format: four 4:5 images, candid flash photography, one visual idea per slide  
Reference mechanism: Rhode Spotwear carousel

1. **Slide 1:** creator surrounded by products. Small text: “I thought I needed more skincare.”
2. **Slide 2:** phone taking the Pore face photo. Small text: “I needed a clearer starting point.”
3. **Slide 3:** creator holding the three products that stayed. Small text: “My routine, after Pore.”
4. **Slide 4:** imperfect bathroom selfie with the routine card visible. Small text: “Early access. Waitlist in bio.”

Do not turn every slide into an infographic. Let the caption explain that Pore combines visible cues with user-provided goals, preferences, and products and is not a diagnostic tool.

## Ready-to-post carousel 2: “three good products, one bad schedule”

Format: six 4:5 slides, more educational and saveable

1. “Three good products. One confusing routine.”
2. Show the three actual product labels and active ingredients.
3. “The problem was not the products.”
4. Show Pore’s timing/compatibility explanation.
5. Show the revised night-one/night-two plan.
6. “Save the schedule. Join Pore’s early-access waitlist.”

Use only combinations supported by Pore’s safety engine and phrase uncertain guidance as “may be irritating for some people” rather than an absolute danger claim.

## The 30-post validation sprint

### Week 1: find the hook

- Shoot the first five concepts with two different opening lines each.
- Keep the body and CTA nearly identical so the hook is the main variable.
- Post one or two videos per day.
- Pin a plain-language comment explaining what Pore does and does not do.

### Week 2: find the proof moment

- Take the two best hooks and test three different payoffs: visible cues, shelf conflict, and routine simplification.
- Publish two carousels using the same winning ideas.
- Reply to the five most product-intent comments with videos.

### Week 3: test creator transfer

- Give five micro-creators one winning premise each.
- Ask for three posts per creator over a month, not one expensive post.
- Let them write the dialogue in their own voice.
- Require a real Pore capture and a real shelf decision.

### Week 4: scale the format, not just the post

- Recut the winner at 7, 12, and 20 seconds.
- Test a male-grooming version, sensitive-skin version, student-budget version, and “too many actives” version.
- Turn the strongest video into a carousel and the strongest carousel into a creator video.

The first 30 deliverables should total roughly:

- 20 founder/owned-account videos
- 6 carousels
- 4 initial creator posts or collab posts

## Measurement system

Give every concept a code, for example `SKEPTIC-01-HOOK-A`. Give every profile link and creator a unique tracking link.

Track:

- Views
- 3-second hold rate
- Average watch time
- Completion rate
- Shares and saves per 1,000 views
- Product-intent comments per 1,000 views
- Profile visits per 1,000 views
- Waitlist signups per 1,000 views
- Waitlist conversion rate from the tracked landing-page session
- Cost per waitlist signup for paid creator posts

Define a Pore winner relative to Pore’s own recent median:

- At least 2x median views after the same amount of time
- At least 1.5x median share-or-save rate
- No drop in waitlist conversion quality
- Multiple product-intent comments, not only generic reactions

Do not scale a high-view post that produces no profile visits or waitlist signups. Cal AI reported an early 10 installs per 1,000 views, but that is an external app-install reference, not Pore’s expected waitlist benchmark. Pore needs its own baseline.

## Creator pilot

Start with creators whose existing content already contains the Pore moment:

- Acne or skin-progress diaries
- “Project pan” and empties creators
- Skincare shelf and GRWM creators
- Cosmetic-chemistry and ingredient educators
- Male grooming creators
- Budget/Sephora-shopping creators
- Toronto lifestyle and beauty micro-creators for in-person capture

Selection criteria:

1. Consistent recent views matter more than follower count.
2. Comments should contain questions, thanks, product names, and purchase intent.
3. The creator must be comfortable showing a real face photo and real products.
4. Their non-sponsored posts should resemble the proposed integration.
5. Avoid creators whose audience engagement is mostly generic compliments.

Brief them with a premise, not a word-for-word script:

> “Show a real moment where your shelf or routine confuses you. Use Pore to make one clear decision. Your audience should understand the problem before they see the app. End in your own voice with the early-access waitlist.”

Negotiate bundles, define whether paid usage/whitelisting is included, and require clear sponsorship disclosure. Track performance per post rather than averaging the creator’s entire month.

## Product and brand changes that would make sharing easier

Pore already has a strong scan animation and result sheet. The highest-leverage additions for growth would be:

1. A shareable “routine changed” card with no private photo by default.
2. A clean before/after routine card: “7 products -> 3 steps.”
3. A same-lighting progress comparison template.
4. A product-fit card: “Keep / pause / alternate / ask a professional.”
5. A creator-friendly demo mode populated with safe sample data.
6. A post-scan prompt that asks whether the user wants to share the routine, not the face analysis.

These outputs should feel like useful social artifacts, not diagnostic reports or attractiveness scores.

## Non-negotiable safety and trust rules

- Say “visible cues,” “cosmetic guidance,” and “routine fit,” not diagnosis.
- Do not promise that one photo understands a person’s skin completely.
- Do not invent product conflicts, results, testimonials, waitlist scarcity, or user counts.
- Match lighting, angle, distance, camera, expression, and time of day in progress comparisons.
- Do not retouch “before” images to intensify redness, texture, pores, or acne.
- Avoid attractiveness scores and shame-based “fix your face” framing.
- Avoid ingredient fearmongering; dose, formulation, frequency, and individual sensitivity matter.
- Get explicit consent before showing anyone’s face photo, results, or products.
- Use a visible sponsorship/affiliate disclosure when a creator is compensated.
- Route possible medical concerns to a qualified professional.

UMAX demonstrates why this line matters: a shareable score can grow quickly and still damage trust or wellbeing. Pore can keep the shareability while making the shareable object a routine decision or progress habit rather than a judgment of the person.

## Recommended first three posts

1. **The skeptical shelf test** because it demonstrates Pore’s complete loop in under 15 seconds.
2. **The friend who buys every viral serum** because it is highly sendable and speaks to wasted money without making a medical claim.
3. **The four-slide “I thought I needed more skincare” carousel** because Rhode proves that a simple, human, launch-timed carousel can massively outperform ordinary brand content.

Shoot all three from the same real creator session. That produces one face-photo clip, shelf B-roll, reaction footage, a routine card, candid stills, and enough material for multiple hook variants.
